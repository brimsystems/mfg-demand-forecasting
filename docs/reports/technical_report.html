Section 2

Training Data

The demand forecasting model learns from the shop's weekly usage of each of its 1,300 stocked items. Each training row is one item on one forecast date. Its features describe the item's usage up to that date, and its target is the usage over the following lead time, rounded to whole weeks.

The historical data is split by date and never shuffled. The first 52 weeks of history (through 2023) are used only to build the rolling features, because every forecast needs a full year of history behind it, so the first usable forecast date is January 2024. The 2024 data was used for tuning, split into a January to June training window and a July to December validation window on which the three candidates were compared. Once the winner was chosen, it was refit on all of 2024 before being scored on 2025 data. We chose to use the full 2025 year of data for model testing, because the bias correction for each demand pattern and the safety-buffer multiples are calibrated on its errors, and both need a full year to reflect the seasonal demand of certain items.

The first six months of 2026 are the model's live output. In live use the model is retrained monthly on every forecast date whose outcome is already known, which by June 2026 spans 2024 through early 2026. Because 2025 serves both to calibrate the model and to report held-out accuracy, the fully out-of-sample test is the live performance from January to June 2026.

Weekly Usage by Split

The model's 20 features fall into three groups. The first is Rolling Usage, including usage windows (4, 13, 26 and 52 weeks), the count of weeks with any usage, the time since the last usage and the recent variability. The second is Calendar Features, to let the model learn seasonal demand patterns. The third is Item Attributes, including cost, lead time, demand pattern, value class and category, to tell the model how to read each item's usage history: the lead time sets how long a window it is forecasting, the demand pattern how far recent usage can be trusted, and the cost, value class and category how similar items tend to behave. The correlation figures presented are calculated as Spearman rank correlations on the training rows, which suit the skewed, zero-heavy usage.

The rolling usage windows are strongly correlated with one another, since they overlap in time. The weeks-since-last-usage and variability features move against the usage windows, as expected for intermittent items. The target is heavily right-skewed: most lead-time windows use a few dozen units, while fasteners and consumables run to thousands. The model is therefore trained on log(1 + usage), so the few very high-volume items do not dominate the fit and the model learns proportional patterns across items of very different sizes. Model Selection & Performance

Section 3.1

Model Selection

Three candidates, a ridge regression, a random forest and an XGBoost gradient-boosted model, were each tuned with Optuna (12 trials per model) on the validation weeks using weighted absolute percentage error (WAPE) as the objective. The models were then refit on train plus validation (i.e., full year 2024) and scored once on the held-out 2025 year. The random forest had the lowest validation error and was selected.

The best random forest model has 400 trees with a maximum depth of 11 and at least 8 rows per leaf. The relatively large leaf-size floor keeps the model from fitting individual spikes and instead learning the items' typical usage patterns.

Section 3.2

Model Performance

Accuracy is measured as WAPE: the total absolute gap between forecast and actual usage across all forecasts, divided by total actual usage. Results are shown for the validation weeks used in tuning, the held-out 2025 year used for selection and calibration, and the live January to June 2026 forecasts, which are the honest test of the model in use. The reorder points use the bias-corrected forecast (Section 5), which trades a little accuracy (44.1% to 45.7% on the held-out year) for forecasts that do not run systematically low.

A live WAPE of 46% means that for every 100 units an item used over its lead time, the forecast was off by about 46 units in either direction. That is typical for item-level forecasts of lumpy, job-driven demand, where a single job or spare-parts order can double an item's usage in a week. The errors largely cancel across items: total forecast usage was within 0.5% of actual. The model's gain over the best simple method (50%) is modest, as is usual for forecasts built from usage history alone. Most of the operational improvement comes from how the forecast is used, covered in Section 5.

The learning curve refits the selected model on growing random shares of the training rows. The held-out error flattens well before the full training set, so more of the same history would add little; better inputs, such as the quantities already on booked jobs, would.

Learning Curve

Calibration compares the mean forecast with the mean actual usage within each tenth of the forecasts, from smallest to largest. Points on the diagonal mean a forecast of, say, 50 units really does average about 50 units of usage. After the bias correction described in Section 5, the forecasts track actual usage closely from about 20 units up, in both the held-out year and live use. For the smallest forecasts, actual usage runs above the forecast (an item forecast at 2 units averages about 3), which the safety buffer absorbs.

Section 4

Feature Importance (SHAP)

SHAP values measure each feature's average contribution to a forecast, here on a sample of the held-out 2025 forecasts and in units of log usage. The model leans most on usage in the same weeks last year, supplier lead time and the last 13 weeks of usage. Last year's usage over the same weeks is by far the strongest, because it is the only feature measured over exactly the window being forecast: the same number of weeks as the item's lead time, at the same time of year. Every other usage feature covers a fixed span (4, 13, 26 or 52 weeks) that the model has to rescale. More recent usage then adjusts that starting point up or down, and the item's demand pattern tells the model how reliably this can be trusted.

Calendar and category features contribute little. About 30% of items are seasonal, but each peaks at its own time of year, and last year's same-weeks usage already carries each item's own seasonal timing; a shared week-of-year or month feature adds little on top of it. Category adds little for a similar reason, since cost, usage level and demand pattern already capture most of what distinguishes the categories.

Section 5

From Forecast to Reorder Decision

The model makes one prediction: the usage forecast, how many units each item will use over its supplier lead time. The reorder point and order quantity presented in the ERP are then calculated from that usage forecast using the rules below.

ElementRule
Reorder pointForecast usage over the lead time, less scheduled usage already visible, plus safety buffer
Fill-rate targetsProduction items 99%, spare parts 98.5%, shop supplies 98.5%
Safety bufferk × σ, with k the smallest multiple whose expected shortfall per cycle, on the model's own error distribution, fits (1 - fill target) × order quantity
σ (uncertainty, in units)The typical size of the gap between the usage forecast and actual usage over the item's lead time (one standard deviation): √(forecast error² + (daily usage × lead-time standard deviation)²). The forecast error is the standard deviation of the item's own 2025 forecast misses, scaled to its lead time and reduced for the share of demand visible on booked jobs; the second term is the variability of the supplier's delivery time
Order-book visibilityReleased jobs about 34 days ahead; booked jobs about 76 days ahead.
Released: a job's parts come off the shelf when it is completed, and the ERP sees the job from its release date, so 34 days is the median time from release to completion of 2025 jobs that finished on time (delayed jobs are excluded, since their extra days are waiting on material). Booked: adds the time from booking the customer order to releasing the job. The records carry no booking date, so this is an assumption: jobs booked four to eight weeks before release, a median of about 42 days.
Order quantityEconomic lot: √(2 × annual usage × $35 per order line ÷ (25% holding × unit cost)), kept between 14 and 120 days of usage.
Annual usage: the model's usage forecast, at a yearly rate. Unit cost: standard cost from the item master. $35 per order line (assumption): the fully loaded cost of placing and processing one line (buyer time, receiving and inspection, putaway, invoice matching); published estimates for manufacturers run roughly $20 to $100+ per purchase order, less per line. 25% holding (assumption): the usual 20 to 30% rule of thumb covering cost of capital, storage, handling, insurance and obsolescence. 14 to 120 days (judgment): no item ordered more often than about every two weeks, and no more than four months of a cheap item at once, below the buyers' old 135-day lot. Supplier minimum order quantities, price breaks and freight are not modelled.
Change thresholdA reorder point moves only when the new value differs by more than 20%: 98% of item-weeks would otherwise change, 7% do
Lead timesRefreshed monthly from each item's last six months of receipts
Inbound ordersPushed back when not yet needed; never on production items

Bias correction. Trained on log usage, the model's back-transformed forecasts estimate the median rather than the mean, so they run low, most of all for intermittent items. Each pattern's forecasts are then scaled by the ratio of actual to forecast usage in the held-out 2025 year. This correction removes almost all of the systematic under-forecasting in live use, as shown below.

Order quantity. The recommended order quantity for each item, calculated from its usage forecast, balances the cost of placing an order line against the cost of holding stock. Expensive, heavily used items are ordered every few weeks, and cheap items a few times a year.

The chart below groups items by value class. The classes are set by annual usage value (units used × unit cost): A items are those that together make up the first 80% of usage value, B items the next 15% and C items the last 5%. In 2025 the typical A item used about $11.5K of stock a year (most above $5.4K) at a median unit cost of $36; a typical B item about $3.1K a year at $12; and a typical C item about $673 a year at $2. The A items, where most of the money sits, are bought in the smallest lots. See the ML Model Overview report for more detail on this order quantity approach.

Order Quantity in Days of Usage, by Value Class

Safety buffer. The buffer is calculated as k standard errors of the item's own forecast error: k × σ, where σ combines the forecast's miss over the lead time with the variability of the supplier's delivery time, and k sets how many of those standard errors to hold. Two refinements are important to highlight here. First, the part of an item's demand already visible on booked production jobs carries no forecast error, so the error is scaled down by that share. Second, k is not taken from a normal curve. It is set on the model's actual 2025 errors so that the expected shortfall per replenishment cycle stays within the fill-rate target for the item's criticality group. The errors have much fatter tails than a normal curve: at k = 2 the expected shortfall is 2.6 times what a normal distribution implies, and at k = 3, 6.9 times. This is important to ensure an adequate buffer size. Because the shortfall allowance scales with the order quantity, an item ordered in large lots needs less buffer (its own lot protects most of the cycle), and an item ordered often needs more.

Section 6

Known Limitations

  • Forecasts from history. The forecast uses past usage only; booked jobs are netted afterward rather than fed in as a feature. Adding the booked quantities as an input is the clearest route to a more accurate forecast for production items.
  • Irreducible item-level error. Lumpy and intermittent demand carries randomness no model can learn; much of the item-level WAPE of 46% reflects that rather than the fit.
  • Fill rate, not events. The buffer targets the share of units supplied. It does not directly target the probability of a stockout event or of a job being held, though these important metrics are closely monitored.
  • Supply is taken as given. Lead times are refreshed from recent receipts and their variability enters the buffer, but late deliveries are not forecast.
  • Order-book assumption. The records carry no booking date for customer orders; jobs are assumed booked four to eight weeks before release.
  • Simulated data. The model is trained and evaluated on synthetic ERP data with embedded patterns and deliberate noise. Real-world accuracy depends on the signal in the shop's own data.

Deployment & Operations

How the model runs in production and where its inputs come from.

SpecificationDetail
Scoring cadenceWeekly (Monday): a usage forecast, reorder point and order quantity for all 1,300 stocked items; the ERP checks every item against its point daily
RetrainingMonthly, on the first forecast date of each month, with the tuned hyperparameters held fixed; a full re-tune of the three candidates on each annual review or retraining trigger
Run timeThe weekly refresh scores 1,300 items in seconds; the monthly retrain of a 400-tree forest on about 52,000+ rows takes about a minute
Outputsrop_schedule.json: per item and week, the reorder level, safety stock, forecast, horizon, reorder point and order quantity, loaded into the ERP reorder queue
Data lineageERP and WMS extracts → dbt staging, intermediate and data-quality models → weekly usage and item attribute marts → demand_model.py (train, forecast, policy) → rop_schedule.json → ERP reorder queue
Retraining triggersJudged on matured months (at least 80% of forecasts scored). Investigate when live WAPE is above its 2025 level, overall or for any demand pattern. Full re-tune when overall WAPE is more than 3 points above the 2025 reference, WAPE for a demand pattern is more than 5 points above its 2025 level, or corrected bias, overall or for a pattern, is outside ±10%. Safety buffers are recalibrated when achieved fill rate is more than 1 point below target for a criticality group; stockout events or jobs held for material above the 2025 monthly average call for investigation.
